Over the course of my 30 plus year career, I have had many different roles ---
individual contributor and manager, team lead and scrum master, developer and
architect. I have worked for large companies, medium-sized companies and small
startups. I've mostly done back-end work, but I've also dabbled with front-end
Javascript over the years (_I definitely prefer the former to the latter!_). One
choice every developer will need to make at some point is whether they want to
remain an individual contributor or move into a management role. It is a choice
which isn't permanent --- you can do management for a while and then go back to
being an individual contributor, or vice versa. I have done this several times
in fact, trying management, getting bored, back to IC, then management again,
then upper management managing managers, back to IC. But as you grow in your
career, "individual contributor" often is "team lead" --- you're doing many aspects
of what a manager does, but without things like performance reviews and hiring/firing
decisions.

The reason I call this out specifically is that "IC" doesn't mean you're a "developer"
per se. You're not coding all day as you're primary activity. With more experience
comes less programming, in many respects. You're spending more time coordinating
across teams, working with product managers, interacting with customers, helping
with recruiting, and other important activities, just not coding. And this is the
point where you're often considered a "software architect", if not in title in
role.

This may be exactly what you want. Maybe "remaining technical" but not coding
is exactly where you want to go. It's a great option, particularly if you enjoy
interacting with a variety of people in a variety of roles. In fact, it's exactly
what I wanted to do, I realized. I had the option many times to focus on management,
and there are many positive and fulfilling aspects to that role. But I found that
it didn't necessarily play to my strengths, and I was happiest when I was working
as a technical leader, helping to guide the direction of the product and the team
without the extra responsibilities of management. And I was good at it. I was
able to bridge the divide between business and technical, communicating the
technical stuff to the business folks and the business stuff to the technical
folks.

But as time went on, I was doing less and less serious coding. I still knew the
ins and outs of the systems I was responsible for, but the number of commits I
was making, and the number and kinds of changes I was making, was smaller and
more targeted. I would be doing bug fixes, or proof of concept work, or prototyping.
No one ever does 100% coding even as a new grad ... there are always meetings.
But the last 4-6 years of my career, I was coding maybe 25% and that was the bug
fixing and prototyping I described. At some point it was even at _most_ 10% coding.
My time was taken up coordinating, strategizing, planning, communicating ---
all necessary and important, but not coding. I was still a technical contributor,
but just not contributing by coding.

Again, this may be exactly what you want. But be prepared. Because one thing may
happen that makes your life a little challenging, if you're trying to find a new
role at a new company. As a senior software architect, where you've spent less and
less time coding, while also seeing your salary go up and up, you may find that
the opportunities for you are fewer and fewer. A software architect with 20 or 30
years of experience and a commensurate salary limits the number of companies that
have roles for you. And if you're willing and trying to find a role where're you're
more hands-on programming, you may find that your coding chops have atrophied a
bit.
